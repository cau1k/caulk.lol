I want more people contributing to open source. I've benefited too much from it to pretend the door should close behind me.

But I want to know what we're asking of the people already maintaining it. An agent can help someone produce a patch. Someone still has to establish whether it works, whether it belongs, and who will deal with it when it breaks.

DHH's recent essay, "[Let the agents democratize open source](https://world.hey.com/dhh/let-the-agents-democratize-open-source-9fd630a9)," leaves very little room for that concern.

I was never big into open source. I benefited from it, but contributing felt like something for people with more knowledge, willpower, and social energy than me. Over the past two years, software developed in the open has helped me find my passion for it again. Tools built to reach everyone, including people who aren't going to pay for an enterprise subscription. It's honestly beautiful. Never would I have expected to fall in love with functional programming, let alone shipping my own contributions to society.

---

What I do want to argue is that shipping software is a completely different domain than writing software. It's like comparing metallurgical artists to Navy Hull Maintenance Technicians. At these two professions' cores, they deal with the same core mechanism of welding. That's not to say the latter profession couldn't be an artist, nor couldn't an artist be a Navy welder, but they acquire separate specializations that take years and years of practice and experience.

What I'm trying to say is that shipping software is a craft. Writing the code is part of it. So is figuring out whether that code belongs in a project other people depend on.

A patch can fix the problem I'm having and still be wrong for the project. Say my agent fixes a file-watching bug on Linux. It passes the tests I ran, so I open a PR. But the project also supports macOS, and the fix depends on behavior that only exists on Linux. Someone has to catch that, explain it, and work out a solution that supports both. If I haven't checked the project's requirements, I've left that discovery to the maintainer.

My agent saving me an afternoon doesn't tell us whether I've saved anyone else any time. I might have just moved the afternoon onto someone who never agreed to spend it.

This can happen with handwritten code too. Agents don't invent careless contributors. But making patches easier to produce doesn't, by itself, establish that the people reviewing them can absorb more of them. Agents may help with review as well. Someone still has to decide what evidence is sufficient to accept a change and take responsibility for that decision.

## What can a maintainer reasonably ask for?

I think a blanket restriction makes the most sense here. "Judge every contribution on its merits" sounds reasonable until you remember that judging it is the work we're talking about. Someone (often an agent, first) has to read the diff, check the claims, and find out whether the contributor understands what they've submitted. Requiring maintainers to do that before they can say no leaves them with the same burden.

Of course this excludes some good contributions. Someone can use an agent, understand the patch, test it properly, and stick around through review. Someone else can handwrite a mess and disappear. That's a real cost of a blanket rule. I still think it's a reasonable tradeoff for a project trying to keep its review workload manageable. Maintainers don't owe us an individual assessment of every patch we can produce.

Suppose a project repeatedly receives submissions whose authors can't explain them or respond usefully to review. The maintainers spend their available time investigating patches that go nowhere. A blanket restriction gives them a policy they can point to without reopening the argument for every submission. People can ignore it or conceal their use of agents, so it won't eliminate the problem. It does establish what work the project is willing to accept.

I support that boundary. The freedom to modify a project doesn't make its maintainers available to review my modification, even if I think it's good. I can use my changes myself or maintain a fork. Getting them included upstream means working within that project's contribution rules.

## And Nietzsche?

DHH invokes _ressentiment_ to explain the backlash: developers resent newcomers getting access to abilities they spent years acquiring. He dismisses concerns about quality and attribution as excuses for protecting that status.

Some developers probably do feel that way. I'm sure it sucks to build your identity around something that suddenly becomes easier for everyone else. But pointing to a contribution restriction doesn't establish that this is why it exists. You still have to look at what happened in the project and what accepting those contributions asked of the people maintaining it.

Otherwise, any boundary becomes evidence of insecurity. A maintainer says they can't handle the review burden, and the answer is that they're afraid of becoming irrelevant. How are they supposed to demonstrate a practical limit if you've already decided their explanation is dishonest?

DHH's argument needs to distinguish between people defending exclusivity and people deciding how much work they can take on. His essay gives us very little basis for doing that.

---

I want people to have the way into open source that I found. Agents can help with that. But contributing starts with respecting what a project is willing to accept. If it doesn't accept agent-generated patches, I don't get to demand an exception because mine is supposedly worth their time.

Where those patches are welcome, I should be able to explain what mine does, show how I checked it, and work through the review. Broader access to software is worth celebrating. The people maintaining it still get to set limits on the work they take on.
