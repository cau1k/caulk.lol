I want more people contributing to open source. I've benefited too much from it to pretend the door should close behind me.

But I want to know what we're asking of the people already maintaining it. An agent can help someone produce a patch. Someone still has to establish whether it works, whether it belongs, and who will deal with it when it breaks.

DHH's recent essay, "[Let the agents democratize open source](https://world.hey.com/dhh/let-the-agents-democratize-open-source-9fd630a9)," leaves very little room for that concern.

I was never big into open source. I benefited from it, but contributing felt like something for people with more knowledge, willpower, and social energy than me. Over the past two years, software developed in the open has helped me find my passion for it again. Tools built to reach everyone, including people who aren't going to pay for an enterprise subscription. It's honestly beautiful. Never would I have expected to fall in love with functional programming, let alone shipping my own contributions to society.

---

Now, I could argue that, by definition, free and open doesn't actually mean "free" or "open," but I tend to dislike semantic arguments made by others. So I'll just stop myself there.

What I do want to argue is that shipping software is a completely different domain than writing software. It's like comparing metallurgical artists to Navy Hull Maintenance Technicians. At these two professions' cores, they deal with the same core mechanism of welding. That's not to say the latter profession couldn't be an artist, nor couldn't an artist be a Navy welder, but they acquire separate specializations that take years and years of practice and experience.

What I'm trying to say is that shipping software is a craft; writing the code that makes up software is another craft of its own, but isn't mission critical per se.
