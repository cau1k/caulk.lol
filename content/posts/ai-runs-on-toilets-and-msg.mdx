The semiconductor supply chain is expansive, complex, and fragile.

Most people know the headline version by now. TSMC manufactures the important chips. ASML makes the impossible lithography machines. Taiwan is geopolitically scary. America wants fabs in Arizona. China wants fewer export controls. Nvidia wants every available square inch of advanced packaging capacity.

All true.

But that version is also too clean for the real story. It makes the whole system sound like a simple diagram where wafers go in one side, GPUs come out the other, and one or two scary companies sit in the middle. The actual semiconductor supply chain is more like a skill tree where every branch has its own weird little boss fight. Ceramic plates from a toilet company. Organic chemistry from the MSG people. Optical mirrors so smooth that the largest bump would disappear under a scaled-up map of Germany. Software licenses expensive enough to make Adobe look like a good company.

The funny part is that this makes the system both stronger and weaker than the usual story suggests.

Stronger, because no single actor can casually recreate the whole thing. There is too much accumulated craft, too much process knowledge, too many suppliers, too many boring industrial miracles buried three layers down in the bill of materials.

Weaker, because a shocking number of those miracles are supplied by one company, one factory, one process family, or one tiny cluster of people who have spent decades becoming unreasonably good at one extremely specific thing.

## The short version

If you only care about the shape of the argument, it is this:

1. Advanced chips depend on extreme specialization.
2. Extreme specialization creates natural monopolies.
3. Those monopolies are often hidden far below the famous brands.
4. The famous brands are still important, but they don't really pull their weight.
5. AI data centers are now bidding against every other industry for the same tiny set of specialized inputs.

So yes, TSMC, ASML, and Taiwan are all incredibly important.

But so is TOTO, Ajinomoto, Zeiss, TRUMPF, Tokyo Electron, Applied Materials, Lamb Research, KLA, Shin-Etsu, SUMCO, SK Hynix, Cadence, Synopsys, and a small army of companies nobody outside semiconductor manufacturing thinks about unless something catches fire (which happens).

## The mistake in the normal chip story

The normal chip story starts too late.

It starts at the fab. Maybe it starts at TSMC. If the person telling it is trying to sound serious, it starts at ASML. Then it gestures vaguely at EUV lithography, mentions Taiwan, says something about export controls, and calls it a day.

That is fine for a 30-second explanation. It also skips over the part that makes semiconductors feel less like normal manufacturing and more like industrialized lab work.

Drawing tiny patterns on silicon is obviously difficult. Doing it over and over again, at industrial scale, with acceptable yields, across thousands of steps, is the real problem. The materials cannot be dirty. The tools cannot drift. The process window cannot wander too far in either direction. A tiny defect can turn a multi-billion dollar fab into a very expensive room full of scrap.

The machines matter, obviously. So do the chemicals, clean rooms, process recipes, inspection tools, packaging lines, design software, supplier field engineers, and the people who know which knob to turn when the line starts behaving like a haunted printer.

The fab is where all of those dependencies finally meet.

By the time a wafer enters it, most of the important constraints are already baked into the tools, materials, recipes, and supplier relationships around it.

## The wafer has to be held by something

Start with something stupidly basic: how do you hold a silicon wafer?

A 300 mm wafer is thin, polished, fragile, expensive, and about to be thrown through a sequence of violent industrial processes. It will be heated, cooled, etched, cleaned, coated, exposed, measured, and moved around at speeds and precision levels that are deeply rude to normal mechanical engineering.

You cannot just clamp it.

At these scales, a mechanical clamp can warp the wafer. That tiny warp matters, because the chip features are measured in nanometers. You also cannot glue it down, because whatever dissolves the glue later can contaminate or destroy the wafer. And whatever holds the wafer has to work through temperature swings, pressure changes, chemical exposure, plasma environments, and mechanical acceleration without putting uneven stress into the silicon.

This is one of those problems that sounds easy only if you do not understand it.

The answer is the electrostatic chuck: a highly engineered ceramic plate that uses electrostatic force to hold the wafer in place. It has to be absurdly flat. It has to be thermally stable. It has to barely expand when heated. It has to be compatible with extreme process environments. Some versions include precisely machined channels for cooling, including liquid helium, because the wafer may need to stay controlled while the surrounding process chamber is doing something insane.

These chucks can cost six figures each.

And one of the companies that got very good at making them is TOTO.

Yes, that TOTO.

The toilet company.

This is not because toilets are secretly semiconductors. It is because TOTO spent a very long time becoming extremely good at ultra-pure ceramics. That expertise turned out to be useful in one of the most technically demanding manufacturing processes on Earth. The path from premium bathroom fixtures to wafer handling is weird, but it is not random.

That is the pattern repeated everywhere in semiconductors. A company becomes world-class at some narrow material or process for one market, then that skill becomes indispensable somewhere much more important. Suddenly the AI boom is competing with hotels and rich people bathrooms for ceramic expertise.

There is something beautiful about that.

Also very dumb.

## The MSG company under your GPU

The next weird bottleneck is Ajinomoto.

Most people know Ajinomoto, if they know it at all, from MSG. Monosodium glutamate. The thing that makes cheap ramen and a lot of good food taste better, despite decades of dumb cultural panic.

Ajinomoto also makes one of the most important materials in advanced chip packaging: ABF, short for Ajinomoto Build-up Film.

ABF is an insulating film used in high-performance chip substrates. If you look at a modern CPU or GPU package, the actual silicon die is not just floating in space. It sits on a substrate, a carefully layered structure that routes electrical signals between the die and the outside world. That substrate has to support increasingly dense interconnects, higher power, tighter pitches, and more layers as chips get more complex.

ABF works because it has a very specific combination of properties:

- it is a stable dielectric
- it can support extremely fine patterning
- it bonds well with copper plating
- it can be built up in layers without becoming useless garbage

That last point is more important than it sounds. Advanced accelerators do not just need a little green square under the die. They need dense, multi-layer substrates that can move enormous amounts of signal and power without turning the package into a failure generator.

This is where Ajinomoto's organic chemistry expertise matters. The company did not stumble into this because someone in a boardroom said "AI is hot, pivot to substrates." It spent decades building chemical process knowledge. ABF is one of the results.

Then other companies, like Ibiden and Unimicron, take films like ABF and turn them into the actual package substrates. That means layering, copper patterning, microvia drilling, lamination, inspection, and a bunch of other processes where the tolerance stack-up gets nasty fast.

So when people talk about GPUs, they usually talk about Nvidia, CUDA, TSMC, and maybe HBM. Fine. But there is a quiet layer underneath that conversation where the whole thing depends on the chemical properties of a film made by the MSG company.

The next time someone says the AI boom is just software eating the world, remember that the world is being eaten through a laminated dielectric film.

## ASML is famous for a reason

ASML gets the attention because ASML deserves a lot of attention.

The company makes the most advanced lithography systems in the world. Lithography is the process that transfers chip patterns onto wafers, and the bleeding edge uses extreme ultraviolet light, usually called EUV. The most advanced EUV tools are some of the most complicated production machines ever built.

A modern ASML EUV scanner contains thousands of supplier inputs. German lasers from TRUMPF. Zeiss optics polished to disgusting precision. Vacuum systems. Power supplies. Stages that move with ridiculous speed and control. Sensors and control loops everywhere. The thing is so large and complicated that shipping one requires multiple cargo planes and teams of engineers to reassemble it inside the fab.

The actual EUV light source is even more ridiculous.

A high-powered CO2 laser fires at droplets of molten tin tens of thousands of times per second. The tin becomes plasma at temperatures around hundreds of thousands of degrees Celsius. That plasma emits extreme ultraviolet light. Then that light is bounced through mirrors because EUV cannot be handled like normal visible light. Lenses do not work the same way. Absorption is brutal. Everything is hostile.

The mirrors are made by Zeiss SMT and are among the smoothest manufactured surfaces on Earth. ASML and Zeiss love the Germany analogy: scale the mirror up to the size of Germany and the biggest bump would still be under a millimeter tall.

This is insane.

Not marketing-insane. Actually insane.

ASML is also not literally the only company that makes lithography machines. Nikon and Canon still make semiconductor lithography tools too. The difference is that ASML is the only company currently building the cutting-edge EUV systems needed for the most advanced single-digit nanometer logic production.

That distinction matters. Most chips are not made on the bleeding edge. Toasters, routers, cars, medical devices, power controllers, washing machines, industrial sensors, and a horrifying amount of critical infrastructure use older process nodes. Those tools can come from ASML's lower-end product lines, Nikon, Canon, and other equipment classes.

So the real point is not "ASML makes all lithography." It does not.

The point is that ASML owns the frontier.

And the frontier is where the money, geopolitics, and AI panic currently live.

## The Big Five problem

Lithography gets the press because lasers are cool and geopolitics is easy content. But a fab full of only lithography machines is not a fab.

It is a very expensive way to not make chips.

Actual chip manufacturing requires deposition, etching, ion implantation, cleaning, polishing, inspection, metrology, thermal processing, resist coating, development, and a large number of steps that sound boring until you realize any one of them can destroy yield.

This is where the semiconductor equipment Big Five come in:

- ASML
- Applied Materials
- Lam Research
- KLA
- Tokyo Electron

Applied Materials makes a huge range of tools used to build chips layer by layer: deposition systems, ion implantation tools, chemical mechanical polishing systems, and other process equipment that sits all over the fab.

Lam Research is dominant in etch, especially plasma etch. Lithography can draw the pattern, but etching is where that pattern gets carved into the material stack. If the etch profile is wrong, if selectivity is wrong, if uniformity is wrong, if the plasma process drifts, the wafer is toast.

KLA dominates inspection and process control. This is the part people underappreciate. You cannot run a modern fab blind. You need metrology and inspection tools constantly telling you whether the process is still inside the narrow corridor where chips work. The earlier you catch defects, the fewer expensive wafers you ruin.

Tokyo Electron, usually TEL, makes coating and developing tools, thermal processing equipment, cleaning systems, and other tools that handle wafers before and after key patterning steps. The coat/develop track around lithography is not glamorous, but if it does not work, your fancy EUV scanner is just generating expensive disappointment.

Together, these companies account for the overwhelming majority of the core tool spend in leading-edge fabs. Each has its own supplier base. Each has its own decades of tacit process knowledge. Each has parts and subsystems that are themselves supplied by companies with narrow expertise.

This is why national self-sufficiency in semiconductors is such a slippery phrase.

Do you mean you can package some chips domestically?

Do you mean you can run a fab domestically using imported tools?

Do you mean you can manufacture the tools domestically?

Do you mean you can manufacture the components inside the tools domestically?

Do you mean you can make the materials, optics, lasers, pumps, ceramics, software, specialty gases, photoresists, mask blanks, and metrology subsystems domestically too?

Because those are very different claims.

Most political conversations blur them together because the honest version is annoying: full-stack semiconductor sovereignty is not a policy announcement. It is a multi-decade industrial civilization project.

Good luck.

## Foundries are not just buildings with machines

Eventually the tools flow into fabs. The most important advanced logic foundries are TSMC, Samsung, Intel, and GlobalFoundries, with TSMC sitting above everyone else at the leading edge.

A foundry takes chip designs from customers and manufactures them. Nvidia, AMD, Apple, Qualcomm, Broadcom, and a long list of other companies design chips, but they do not manufacture their most advanced logic themselves. They send designs to a foundry.

This is weird if you think about it from the outside.

Imagine Ford designing a car, then asking a separate company to manufacture it in a factory that also makes Toyota's car, Honda's car, and maybe a few parts for a direct competitor. That sounds unstable. But for semiconductors it makes sense because the manufacturing side is so expensive and specialized that most design companies would be insane to rebuild it themselves.

A leading-edge fab costs tens of billions of dollars. The tools are brutal. The process recipes take years to mature. Yield learning is its own dark art. Running the fab is not just "buy ASML machine, press chip button."

This is why TSMC's dominance is so hard to copy.

TSMC is not merely a company that owns expensive equipment in Taiwan. It is a company that has spent decades compounding process knowledge, customer trust, supplier relationships, engineering culture, and manufacturing discipline. The fabs are the visible assets. The invisible asset is the accumulated ability to make the fabs produce useful chips at volume.

That is also why overseas expansion is hard.

TSMC can build fabs in Arizona and Japan. It can import tools. It can send engineers. It can transfer recipes. But the highest-end production still clusters in Taiwan because the soft infrastructure is harder to move than the machines.

People hate this answer because it sounds vague.

It is not vague. It is craft.

Every serious manufacturing industry has this. The formal process document tells you what should happen. The experienced operator, process engineer, supplier rep, maintenance team, and yield specialist know what actually happens when the line starts drifting at 2 a.m.

That stuff does not fit nicely in a CHIPS Act press release.

## Raw wafers are also a bottleneck

Before a fab can do anything, it needs the wafer itself.

The wafer starts as high-purity silicon. The process begins with quartz or sand, gets refined into electronic-grade polysilicon, and then pulled into a single crystal ingot through the Czochralski process. That ingot gets sliced into wafers, lapped, polished, cleaned, inspected, and shipped with contamination levels that would make normal manufacturing look like a dumpster fire.

The tolerances are absurd because the wafer is the foundation. If the wafer has defects, impurities, crystal issues, flatness problems, or surface contamination, every later step inherits that problem.

The wafer market is also concentrated. Shin-Etsu Chemical and SUMCO, both Japanese, are two of the largest suppliers. GlobalWafers in Taiwan and Siltronic in Germany are also major players.

This is another place where the industry looks robust from far away and fragile up close.

Multiple companies exist. Multiple countries are involved. This is not literally one factory in one town. Good.

But disruptions still ripple hard because fabs need qualified wafers, not generic circles of shiny silicon. Qualification takes time. Process flows are tuned. Customers are conservative because broken wafers do not create charming outages. They create yield loss, missed shipments, and cars sitting half-finished in lots because some controller chip is missing.

The semiconductor shortage was not one shortage. It was a web of shortages, allocation decisions, demand shocks, fire damage, logistics messes, and planning failures. Wafers were part of that story because wafers sit at the bottom of everything.

You can have the best chip design in the world and the fanciest EUV scanner on Earth.

No wafer, no chip.

## Memory is not a side quest

Logic gets most of the attention because Nvidia GPUs are the public face of the AI boom. But memory is now just as strategic.

A GPU without enough fast memory is a very expensive space heater. Modern AI accelerators need high-bandwidth memory, HBM, packaged close to the compute die. The reason is simple: moving data is the bottleneck. If the compute units cannot read and write fast enough, the theoretical FLOPS number becomes a decorative sticker.

The leading memory players are Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron. Samsung does both logic and memory. SK Hynix and Micron are dedicated memory giants. Together they dominate the memory supply that actually matters for advanced computing.

HBM is especially concentrated. SK Hynix became the key supplier for Nvidia's top accelerators because it was able to deliver advanced HBM at the right performance and volume. That is a very nice position to have until the entire AI infrastructure buildout starts leaning on your packaging skill, yield, and capacity.

HBM is not just DRAM with a better name. It is stacked memory. Multiple DRAM dies are vertically stacked and connected with through-silicon vias, then packaged close to the GPU. The engineering is nasty. Thermal behavior matters. Yield matters. Bonding and assembly matter. The package has to move absurd bandwidth without falling apart.

This makes South Korea one of the quiet centers of the AI boom.

Not because every executive wants to say "memory wall" on earnings calls, but because the actual memory wall is physical. If you cannot build enough HBM stacks and attach them to accelerators, the rest of the AI supply chain gets rate limited.

This is why I find some AI infrastructure discourse so unserious. People will talk about model scaling like the only constraints are GPUs, power, and capital.

Those are constraints.

But there is also a guy somewhere worrying about bonding yield on a stack of memory dies, and that guy may have more control over your frontier training cluster than your favorite AI lab's roadmap.

## Packaging is where the bottleneck gets physical

Once you have the logic die and HBM stacks, you still need to put them together.

This is advanced packaging, and it has become one of the most important bottlenecks in AI hardware. For Nvidia-class accelerators, the relevant term is CoWoS, TSMC's chip-on-wafer-on-substrate packaging technology.

CoWoS allows large logic dies and HBM stacks to sit together on an interposer and substrate so they can communicate at extremely high bandwidth. This is not optional. The package is part of the product. If the packaging capacity is constrained, then GPU supply is constrained even if the logic die and memory exist separately.

For a while, frontier accelerator supply was effectively capped by how fast TSMC could expand CoWoS capacity.

That is the kind of bottleneck finance people understand only after it hits guidance.

Nvidia can design more chips. TSMC can fab more dies. SK Hynix can make more HBM. But if the package capacity is not there, the parts cannot become finished accelerators at the rate customers want.

This is one reason the chip supply chain is hard to reason about. The bottleneck moves.

Sometimes it is EUV scanner availability. Sometimes it is substrates. Sometimes it is HBM. Sometimes it is packaging. Sometimes it is power. Sometimes it is a specialty chemical or a supplier nobody modeled because it lived three levels down in procurement hell.

The industry is a constraint-whack-a-mole machine.

AI just made the mallet bigger.

## The software bottleneck nobody outside chip design wants to think about

Even before manufacturing, someone has to design the chip.

That design process depends on electronic design automation, EDA, software. Modern chips have tens of billions of transistors. You do not draw that by hand like a little silicon mandala. You use specialized tools to describe, simulate, verify, place, route, time, power-optimize, and validate the design before sending it to the fab.

At advanced nodes, the software has to model physical effects that are no longer clean textbook abstractions. Timing, power, thermal behavior, signal integrity, parasitics, manufacturing variation, and quantum-scale weirdness all start becoming practical engineering concerns.

The two dominant EDA companies are Cadence and Synopsys.

Their tools are expensive enough to feel fake. A serious annual license can cost hundreds of thousands of dollars per seat depending on the toolchain and arrangement. A small team can burn millions per year just on design software.

And they pay it.

Because what is the alternative? Rebuild your entire chip design pipeline on untrusted tools and hope your multi-billion dollar tapeout works? Very funny.

EDA is one of the clearest examples of software becoming infrastructure. Not "infrastructure" in the SaaS landing page sense. Actual infrastructure. The entire semiconductor industry relies on these tools to decide whether a chip design is manufacturable before it becomes a physical object.

This also means EDA is geopolitical. Cadence and Synopsys are American companies. The US government can restrict access. Export controls on chip manufacturing are not just about physical machines. They can reach into the software layer that makes the machines useful.

This is the part that should scare anyone trying to build an independent semiconductor stack from scratch.

You do not just need a fab.

You need the design software, the IP ecosystem, the verification flows, the trained engineers, the process design kits, the foundry relationships, and the confidence that when the tool says the design works, it is not lying to you in a $500 million accent.

## Why monopoly is the natural shape here

A lot of these markets look monopolistic or oligopolistic because the customers are lazy only in the same way climbers are lazy for not inventing a new mountain.

The incentives push toward concentration.

If a component has to sit at the edge of physics, the supplier has to spend years or decades learning how to make it. The market may be small in unit count but enormous in value. The customers are conservative because failure is catastrophically expensive. Switching suppliers is slow because qualification is slow. The leading supplier gets more feedback, more revenue, more process data, and more trust. Then it gets even better.

Compounding does the rest.

This is why the semiconductor supply chain is full of companies that appear to have absurdly defensible niches. Some are protected by patents. Some by trade secrets. Some by process knowledge. Some by customer trust. Some by the simple fact that nobody wants to risk a production line on a supplier that is merely "probably fine."

In normal software, switching vendors is annoying.

In leading-edge semiconductor manufacturing, switching vendors can mean requalifying a process that touches billions of dollars of output.

So the incumbent wins, then keeps winning, unless it falls behind badly enough that customers have no choice.

## Why the system is stronger than it looks

The usual doom version says the chip supply chain is fragile because it depends on a few suppliers.

That is true, but incomplete.

The system is also strong because those suppliers are deeply interdependent. ASML needs TSMC and Samsung to buy its tools. TSMC needs ASML, Applied Materials, Lam, KLA, Tokyo Electron, and the rest to keep fabs running. Nvidia needs TSMC, SK Hynix, substrate suppliers, packaging capacity, EDA tools, and a million other things. Ajinomoto needs substrate makers. Zeiss needs ASML. Everyone needs customers with absurd capital budgets.

This creates a weird balance of power.

A supplier may be the only credible seller, but the buyer may also be one of the only credible buyers. Monopoly power meets monopsony power. Nobody is fully free. Everyone is rich enough to be annoying, important enough to be protected, and dependent enough to return the call.

That interdependence is a stabilizing force.

It is not friendship. It is mutual hostage-taking with purchase orders.

The semiconductor industry has spent decades learning how to coordinate across this mess. The process is ugly, but it works. Suppliers co-develop tools with customers. Foundries feed requirements back into equipment makers. Equipment makers drag their suppliers forward. Materials companies tune products for specific process windows. Packaging companies expand capacity around customer roadmaps.

That is not easy to disrupt casually.

It is why the industry can absorb a lot of shocks without instantly collapsing. There are buffers, alternate suppliers in some layers, long-term contracts, inventory strategies, dual-sourcing where possible, and a strong shared incentive not to let the whole thing catch fire.

Again: stronger than it looks.

## Why the system is weaker than it looks

Then the weaker part shows up.

Interdependence does not remove bottlenecks. It often hides them until demand spikes.

AI created a demand spike with money attached. Not vague demand. Real orders, real data centers, real capex, real hyperscalers willing to pay stupid amounts for accelerators. That demand does not only hit Nvidia. It propagates backward into HBM, CoWoS, substrates, ABF, wafer starts, equipment orders, specialty gases, ceramics, optics, photoresists, and engineering labor.

Every layer has a ramp time.

You cannot instantly create more HBM capacity. You cannot instantly train more advanced packaging experts. You cannot instantly build more EUV scanners. You cannot instantly qualify a new substrate supplier. You cannot instantly make another Zeiss. You cannot instantly recreate a Japanese chemical company that has been quietly optimizing one material for decades.

This is where the supply chain becomes fragile.

Not because it has one single point of failure, but because it has many single-ish points of failure. The system can route around some problems. It cannot route around all of them at once, especially when everyone is trying to scale at the same time.

The popular fragility model is a bridge with one weak cable.

The semiconductor model is a machine with 900 parts that each have a six-month to ten-year lead time.

Good luck finding the part that matters before it becomes the part that matters.

## The geopolitics are real, but not sufficient

Taiwan is still the obvious geopolitical risk.

TSMC produces the overwhelming majority of leading-edge logic chips. Taiwan sits in one of the most politically dangerous places on Earth. A conflict around Taiwan would be catastrophic for the semiconductor industry and for basically every downstream industry that depends on advanced chips.

That part is not overhyped.

What is overhyped is the idea that moving the fab solves the problem.

A fab in Arizona reduces some risk. A fab in Japan reduces some risk. More geographic distribution is good. Nobody serious should argue against redundancy.

But moving or duplicating capacity does not automatically duplicate the ecosystem. You still need the tools, process engineers, suppliers, materials, maintenance, spare parts, wafers, packaging, memory, EDA, and tacit knowledge. You also need enough customers willing to run the right products at the right scale to keep the learning loop alive.

A fab is not a flag planted in concrete.

It is a living industrial organism.

The geopolitics matter because governments can move money, restrict exports, subsidize capacity, and force strategic choices. But the industrial reality moves slower than the press conference. That mismatch is where a lot of bad analysis comes from.

You can announce sovereignty in a day.

You build it over decades, if you can build it at all.

## The AI boom is bidding up the weird stuff

The funniest and most important consequence of the AI boom is that it makes strange inputs strategically valuable.

Not just GPUs. Not just electricity. Not just land for data centers.

Ultra-pure aluminum oxide. Advanced ceramics. ABF. Specialty photoresists. Zeiss-grade optical glass. HBM bonding expertise. CoWoS capacity. Mask blanks. Specialty gases. Wafer inspection tools. Vacuum pumps. Power supplies. EDA licenses. Process engineers. Field service teams.

And yes, indirectly, the world's finest Japanese toilets.

This is what happens when one demand curve moves through a tightly coupled industrial stack. The pressure does not stay where the headlines are. It travels.

Everyone wants more AI accelerators. More accelerators need more HBM. More HBM needs more memory capacity and packaging. More packaging needs more substrates and interposers. More substrates need more ABF and substrate manufacturing capacity. More wafers need more equipment. More equipment needs more optics, ceramics, lasers, pumps, power supplies, and metrology.

The supply chain is not a line.

It is a graph, and AI demand is flooding the graph.

## The actual takeaway

The semiconductor supply chain is not fragile in the simple way people usually mean.

It is not one bad day away from disappearing because one company sneezes. The industry has redundancy in some places, deep coordination in others, and enough money flowing through it that problems get attention fast.

But it is fragile in a more interesting way.

It depends on an enormous number of specialized capabilities that cannot be quickly replicated. Some are concentrated because patents protect them. Some because trade secrets protect them. Some because the learning curve is too steep. Some because the market is too small to support five serious competitors. Some because customers cannot afford to experiment.

That means the system can be resilient against ordinary shocks while still being exposed to extraordinary ones.

A factory fire can matter. A war can matter. An export control can matter. A packaging bottleneck can matter. A shortage of one boring material can matter. A missing piece of process knowledge can matter.

The real story is not "globalization bad" or "reshoring good" or "ASML controls the world" or "Taiwan is the only thing that matters."

The real story is that advanced semiconductors are the product of accumulated specialization at planetary scale.

Nobody owns the whole stack.

Nobody can easily leave the stack.

And for now, every new AI data center is going to keep reaching backward through that stack, bidding up everything from Korean memory packaging to Japanese ceramics to Dutch lithography to American design software.

This is the part of the AI boom that is easiest to miss if you only look at model releases and GPU prices. The future is not being bottlenecked by one thing. It is being negotiated across thousands of very specific things.

Some of them are made by companies you have heard of.

Some of them are made by companies you know for ramen and toilets.
