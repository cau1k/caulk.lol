Not everyone can _ship_ code, but anyone can now _~write~ generate_ code. This is the point I want to make in reviewing DHH's recent article, "[Let the agents democratize open source,](https://world.hey.com/dhh/let-the-agents-democratize-open-source-9fd630a9)" which I belive poses a strong, but inherently flawed, argument against a recent push to close down software. He posits that the broader OSS ecosystem shouldn't become more protectionist now that anyone can generate code, especially after all the work done to make free and open software truly free. While the fight for OSS was, without a shadow of a doubt, incredibly necessary, I don't think... (comparison isn't a good comparison just for the fuck of it). It is more akin to the problem many academics face when publishing research though, that fight was lost long, long ago.

Now, I could argue that, by definition, free and open doesn't actually mean "free" or "open," but I tend to dislike semantic arguments made by others. So I'll just stop myself there.

What I do want to argue is that shipping software is a completely different domain than writing software. It's like comparing metallurgical artists to Navy Hull Maintenance Technicians. At these two professions' cores, they typically deal with the same core mechanism of welding. That's not to say the latter profession couldn't be an artist, nor couldn't an artist be a Navy welder, but they acquire separate specializations that take years and years of practice and experience.

What I'm trying to say is that shipping software is a craft; writing the code that makes up software is another craft of its own, but isn't mission critical per se.

---

I was never big into open source. I, and many, many others, have benefited greatly from it, but I was never an intense contributor to the "developers own the means of production" (sarcasm) side of things. Over the past two years, I have managed to reaffirm my passion for software through technology developed in the open, with goals to reach everyone and not just those willing to heft over an enterprise subscription. It's honestly beautiful. Never would I have expected to fall in love with a better implementation of RxJS (I'm talking about [Effect](https://github.com/Effect-TS/effect)). Nor would I have considered myself to (1) have the willpower, (2) be knowledgeable enough, and (3) extroverted enough to contribute my own work that millions of people benefit from every day.
